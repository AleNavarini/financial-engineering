# Volatility, VIX, Futures, and Options: A Practical Learning Guide

## Purpose

This document explains the market concepts behind a possible volatility research project built with LSEG Workspace data, formerly called Refinitiv or Eikon data.

The initial project scope is the Cboe Volatility Index (`VIX`), VIX futures, and related instruments. The goal is to understand what each instrument and data field represents before designing software or a trading model.

This is an educational and research document. It is not investment advice. Futures and options can produce losses larger than the initial cash committed to a position.

## 1. The Main Ideas in One Page

The word **volatility** describes how much prices vary. It does not, by itself, say whether prices move up or down.

There are several different volatility concepts:

- **Historical or realized volatility** measures price changes that already occurred.
- **Implied volatility** is inferred from current option prices and reflects the volatility priced by the options market.
- **The VIX Index** is a specific Cboe calculation based on S&P 500 Index option prices. It represents a 30-day, forward-looking measure of expected U.S. equity-market volatility.
- **VIX futures** are tradable contracts whose final value is determined by a special VIX settlement calculation on a future date.
- **VIX options** are options tied to VIX settlement. Their economics are closely related to the corresponding VIX futures contract, not only to today's spot VIX value.

These values are related, but they are not interchangeable.

For example, suppose:

- Spot VIX is 15.
- The next VIX future is 17.
- The second VIX future is 18.

This does not mean the market is making three contradictory statements. Each number refers to a different object and time horizon:

- Spot VIX measures a constant 30-day expectation starting now.
- The first future refers to the VIX settlement value on its expiration date.
- The second future refers to a later settlement date.

The set of futures prices across expiration dates is the **VIX futures term structure**. This curve is one of the most useful starting points for a volatility project.

## 2. What Volatility Means

### 2.1 Price direction versus price variability

A return measures direction and size. Volatility measures the variability of returns.

An asset that rises by 0.1% every day has positive returns but very low volatility. An asset that alternates between +4% and -4% has a return near zero over two days but high volatility.

Volatility is therefore not the same as:

- A loss
- Market risk in its complete sense
- Trading volume
- The probability that a price will fall
- The size of the price itself

Volatility is one dimension of risk. Liquidity, credit risk, gap risk, leverage, and model risk are other dimensions.

### 2.2 Returns

Volatility is normally calculated from returns rather than raw price changes. A common definition is the logarithmic return:

```text
r_t = ln(P_t / P_(t-1))
```

Where:

- `P_t` is today's price.
- `P_(t-1)` is the previous price.
- `r_t` is the return between those observations.

Simple percentage returns can also be used:

```text
r_t = (P_t / P_(t-1)) - 1
```

For small changes, simple and logarithmic returns are similar. Logarithmic returns are often useful in quantitative work because they add across time.

### 2.3 Realized volatility

Realized volatility describes observed price movement over a past period. A basic daily calculation is:

1. Calculate daily returns.
2. Calculate their sample standard deviation.
3. Annualize the result by multiplying by the square root of the number of trading days in a year.

```text
annualized volatility = standard deviation of daily returns * sqrt(252)
```

The number `252` is a common approximation for U.S. trading days per year. It is a convention, not a natural constant.

Realized volatility depends on choices made by the analyst:

- Return type: simple or logarithmic
- Observation frequency: daily, hourly, or intraday
- Lookback window: 5, 20, 60, or more days
- Annualization convention
- Treatment of overnight moves and missing observations

Two realized-volatility series can differ even when both are valid because they use different definitions.

### 2.4 Implied volatility

An option price depends on several inputs, including:

- Underlying price
- Strike price
- Time to expiration
- Interest rates
- Expected dividends, when applicable
- Expected future variability

If the market option price and other inputs are known, an option-pricing model can solve for the volatility value consistent with that price. This value is called **implied volatility**.

Implied volatility is not a direct forecast generated by one investor. It is a price-derived quantity that reflects supply, demand, risk aversion, hedging needs, market structure, and expectations.

### 2.5 The volatility risk premium

Implied volatility has often exceeded subsequent realized volatility over long samples. The difference is commonly called the **volatility risk premium**.

One simplified definition is:

```text
volatility risk premium = implied volatility - subsequent realized volatility
```

The comparison must use aligned horizons. A 30-day implied measure should be compared with volatility realized over the corresponding future 30-day period.

This premium is not free profit. Sellers of volatility can experience rare and severe losses during market shocks. The premium can be viewed partly as compensation for accepting convex crash risk.

## 3. What the VIX Index Represents

### 3.1 Definition

The Cboe Volatility Index is designed to measure the market's expectation of 30-day forward-looking volatility of the S&P 500 Index. Cboe derives it from a range of S&P 500 Index (`SPX`) call and put option prices.

The VIX is:

- Forward-looking in the sense that it uses current prices for options that expire in the future
- Based on the S&P 500 options market
- A constant 30-day measure
- Expressed in annualized percentage points
- An index, not a stock or fund

If VIX is 20, the market is pricing a 30-day volatility measure equivalent to approximately 20% per year. It does not mean the S&P 500 will fall 20%, and it does not guarantee that realized volatility will equal 20%.

### 3.2 A rough daily-move interpretation

A common approximation converts annualized volatility to a one-day standard deviation:

```text
expected one-day move magnitude ~= VIX / sqrt(252)
```

For VIX at 20:

```text
20% / sqrt(252) ~= 1.26%
```

This is only a scale estimate under simplifying assumptions. It is not a promise that the S&P 500 will stay inside a +/-1.26% range, and actual returns do not follow a perfect normal distribution.

### 3.3 How Cboe constructs VIX

At a high level, Cboe:

1. Selects eligible SPX and SPXW options around the target horizon.
2. Uses near-term and next-term expirations that bracket 30 calendar days.
3. Uses out-of-the-money puts and calls across many strikes.
4. Uses option quote midpoints, subject to filtering rules.
5. Calculates implied variance for each expiration.
6. Interpolates those variances to a constant 30-day horizon.
7. Takes the square root and multiplies by 100 to express the result as a volatility index.

The method is often called **model-free implied variance** because it uses a strip of option prices rather than taking the implied volatility of one at-the-money option. It still depends on methodology and market-data rules.

### 3.4 Why many option strikes are used

One at-the-money option would provide a narrow view of option pricing. VIX uses many out-of-the-money calls and puts to measure a broader market price of variance.

This matters because implied volatility varies by strike. The pattern is called the **volatility smile** or **volatility skew**. For equity indices, downside puts often have higher implied volatility than comparable upside calls because investors pay for crash protection.

### 3.5 Why VIX often rises when stocks fall

VIX and S&P 500 returns often move in opposite directions because:

- Investors buy downside protection during stress.
- Market makers raise option prices when uncertainty and hedging costs increase.
- Large equity moves increase expected future variability.
- Equity declines can occur faster than equity gains, producing an asymmetric response.

This inverse relationship is strong at times, but it is not a fixed mathematical rule. VIX can rise while stocks rise, or fall while stocks fall.

### 3.6 Spot VIX is not directly tradable

VIX is a calculated index. An investor cannot buy one unit of spot VIX in the same way that an investor buys one share of stock.

Tradable exposure comes through instruments such as:

- VIX futures
- VIX options
- Options on VIX futures
- Exchange-traded products that hold or replicate positions in VIX futures

Each instrument has its own price behavior. None should be expected to track spot VIX perfectly.

## 4. The VIX Family

VIX is the best-known member of a larger set of volatility measures. Depending on LSEG entitlements, a research project can include:

- **VIX:** approximately 30-day expected S&P 500 volatility.
- **VIX9D:** a shorter expected-volatility horizon of approximately nine days.
- **VIX3M:** approximately three-month expected volatility.
- **VIX6M:** approximately six-month expected volatility.
- **VIX1Y:** approximately one-year expected volatility.
- **VVIX:** expected volatility of VIX, derived from VIX option prices. It is sometimes described as the volatility of volatility.

The difference between short- and long-horizon volatility indices provides another term structure. For example, a sharp rise in VIX9D relative to VIX3M can indicate that the market is pricing a near-term event or shock.

The exact RIC for each index must be discovered and tested in the UCEMA Workspace account. Do not assume that every displayed Workspace instrument is available through the API.

## 5. What a Futures Contract Is

### 5.1 General futures mechanics

A futures contract is a standardized exchange-traded agreement whose value depends on an underlying reference. Each contract has:

- A standardized contract size
- An expiration date
- A minimum price increment, called the tick size
- Trading hours
- A settlement method
- Margin requirements

Futures positions are marked to market. Gains and losses are reflected in the account as prices change. A futures trader normally posts margin rather than paying the entire notional value at entry.

Margin creates leverage. A relatively small market move can produce a large gain or loss relative to posted collateral.

### 5.2 VIX futures

VIX futures trade on Cboe Futures Exchange under the exchange root `VX`. They are cash-settled contracts based on a future VIX final settlement value.

The standard VIX futures contract has a multiplier of $1,000 per VIX point. A move from 18.00 to 19.00 therefore changes one contract's value by $1,000.

For a standard outright contract, a 0.05-point tick corresponds to $50:

```text
0.05 VIX points * $1,000 = $50
```

Contract specifications can change. Production software should treat exchange reference data as authoritative rather than hard-coding specifications indefinitely.

### 5.3 What a VIX futures price means

A VIX futures price is the market price for exposure to the VIX settlement value on a specified future date.

It is not simply today's VIX plus an interest-rate carrying cost. Unlike a storable commodity, spot VIX cannot be bought and stored until futures expiration. VIX futures pricing reflects expectations, risk premiums, hedging flows, supply and demand, and the mean-reverting behavior of volatility.

If spot VIX is 15 and a three-month VIX future is 19, the future does not guarantee that spot VIX will rise to 19. It says that market participants currently exchange risk around the future settlement at a price of 19.

### 5.4 Expiration and final settlement

VIX futures normally settle in cash. Their final settlement value is determined by a **Special Opening Quotation**, commonly called the SOQ, on the expiration morning, usually a Wednesday.

The VIX settlement symbol is commonly called `VRO` in Cboe materials. The SOQ differs from the continuously published spot VIX calculation. It uses opening prices from a specific strip of SPX or SPXW options with the required expiration.

Consequences:

- The final futures value can differ from the spot VIX quote observed near the same time.
- Settlement-day liquidity and option-opening conditions matter.
- A model must not assume that the prior day's VIX close is the futures settlement value.

### 5.5 Monthly and weekly VIX futures

Cboe lists standard monthly VIX futures and can also list weekly expirations. Weekly contracts provide more precise event timing, while monthly contracts are usually the main reference points for the term structure.

The active set changes over time as contracts expire and new contracts are listed. A project should discover the current chain instead of maintaining a static list.

## 6. The VIX Futures Term Structure

### 6.1 Definition

The term structure is the set of futures prices ordered by expiration date.

Example:

| Contract | Days to expiration | Price |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Spot VIX | Constant 30-day measure | 15.0 |
| Future 1 | 20 | 16.5 |
| Future 2 | 48 | 17.4 |
| Future 3 | 76 | 18.0 |
| Future 4 | 111 | 18.3 |

The shape contains information about how volatility risk is priced across time.

### 6.2 Contango

The curve is in **contango** when later futures are priced above nearer futures, at least for the section being compared.

```text
F2 > F1
```

Contango is common in calm markets. It can reflect the expectation that unusually low volatility will move toward a higher long-run level, as well as compensation demanded by sellers of volatility risk.

Contango is not proof that VIX will rise. The curve can decline over time as contracts approach settlement.

### 6.3 Backwardation

The curve is in **backwardation** when nearer futures trade above later futures.

```text
F1 > F2
```

Backwardation often appears during market stress because immediate volatility is priced above longer-term volatility. It can indicate that the market expects current stress to decline, although the stress can also become worse.

### 6.4 Curve slope

A simple front slope is:

```text
absolute slope = F2 - F1
```

A normalized slope is:

```text
percentage slope = (F2 / F1) - 1
```

Normalization makes observations from different volatility levels easier to compare. For robust comparisons, an analyst can also divide by the days between expiration dates:

```text
daily curve slope = ((F2 / F1) - 1) / (D2 - D1)
```

No single slope definition is universally correct. The project must state its convention.

### 6.5 Spot-futures basis

The basis compares a futures price with spot VIX:

```text
basis = futures price - spot VIX
```

Or in percentage terms:

```text
percentage basis = (futures price / spot VIX) - 1
```

This is informative, but spot VIX and a futures contract refer to different horizons. A large basis is not automatically an arbitrage opportunity.

### 6.6 Calendar spreads

A calendar spread is a position in two futures with different expirations. For example:

- Long the second future
- Short the first future

The position is more directly exposed to changes in curve shape than to the overall VIX level, although the exposures do not cancel perfectly.

Research uses include:

- Measuring curve steepening or flattening
- Studying event risk concentrated in one expiration
- Comparing stress in near and distant horizons

Calendar spreads still have basis, liquidity, settlement, and nonlinear regime risks.

## 7. Convergence, Carry, and Roll Yield

### 7.1 Convergence

As a futures contract approaches expiration, its price and the expected settlement value become increasingly linked. At final settlement, the contract is settled against the SOQ.

Before expiration, futures and spot VIX can remain materially different because spot VIX always represents a new forward 30-day window, while the futures contract points to one fixed settlement date.

### 7.2 Carry

**Carry** is the gain or loss associated with holding a position while time passes, assuming relevant market conditions do not change. In volatility markets, the term is used in several related ways and must be defined precisely.

For a long VIX future in contango, an unchanged volatility environment can produce negative carry as the contract approaches a lower expected settlement level. A short position can benefit in that scenario. During a shock, however, losses on a short volatility position can be sudden and much larger than accumulated carry.

### 7.3 Roll yield

A strategy that maintains continuous futures exposure must replace an expiring contract with a later contract. This process is called **rolling**.

If a long strategy sells a cheaper near contract and buys a more expensive later contract in contango, the roll is economically unfavorable at that moment. In backwardation, a long strategy can sell a higher-priced near contract and buy a lower-priced later contract.

Roll yield is often described too casually. A correct strategy return depends on:

- Daily futures price changes
- The exact roll schedule
- The weight assigned to each contract
- Transaction costs and bid-ask spreads
- Margin and collateral return
- Contract multipliers

Subtracting two prices is useful for describing curve shape, but it is not a complete return calculation.

### 7.4 Why VIX exchange-traded products decay

Many short-term VIX exchange-traded products maintain exposure by rolling from the first future toward the second. Persistent contango can create a recurring drag on long products.

This does not mean every VIX product always falls. During severe volatility shocks, futures prices can rise sharply and the curve can enter backwardation. Leveraged and inverse products have additional daily-reset and compounding effects.

## 8. Futures Identifiers in LSEG

### 8.1 RICs

LSEG uses Reuters Instrument Codes, or **RICs**, to identify instruments.

Likely VIX instruments include:

- `.VIX` for the spot VIX Index
- `0#VX:` for the current VIX futures chain
- `VXc1` for the nearest-expiry continuation series
- `VXc2` for the second continuation series
- Individual contracts built from the `VX` root, a month code, and a year code

These identifiers must be tested in the signed-in UCEMA Workspace account. Data availability and exact identifiers can differ by service, history period, and entitlement.

### 8.2 Futures month codes

Futures use standard month letters:

| Month | Code | Month | Code |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| January | F | July | N |
| February | G | August | Q |
| March | H | September | U |
| April | J | October | V |
| May | K | November | X |
| June | M | December | Z |

For example, an individual September 2026 VIX futures RIC can use the form `VXU6`, subject to the active LSEG convention.

### 8.3 Chain RICs

A chain RIC represents a changing list of related instruments. The active VIX futures chain is `0#VX:`.

The chain should be the primary source for discovering currently listed contracts because:

- Contracts expire.
- New contracts are listed.
- Weekly contracts can appear.
- Static contract lists become stale.

LSEG states that the active VIX chain changed on September 10, 2012. Historical work before that date may require the older `0#VX:VE` convention or specific expired-contract RICs.

### 8.4 Continuation RICs

Continuation RICs such as `VXc1` provide a convenient time series that automatically changes its underlying contract.

Advantages:

- Easy charting
- Simple regime comparison
- No need to select a new symbol after every expiration

Limitations:

- The underlying contract changes at roll points.
- Price jumps can reflect contract replacement rather than a market return.
- A continuation series does not automatically reproduce a tradable rolling strategy.
- Different continuation methods can roll by expiry, volume, or open interest.

For serious backtesting, store individual contract histories and implement an explicit roll rule.

### 8.5 Expired contracts

Expired contracts are essential for correct historical curve reconstruction. LSEG can use decade suffixes for old futures RICs. The correct symbol format should be discovered from Workspace or LSEG reference data rather than guessed.

A historical database should preserve:

- The original contract RIC
- Expiration date
- Observation date and time
- Settlement price
- Volume and open interest
- Whether the value is preliminary or final, if known

- No trade occurred recently.
- The instrument uses a different field.
- The market is closed.

### 9.1 Bid, ask, and midpoint

The bid-ask spread is:

```text
spread = ask - bid
```

The quote midpoint is:

```text
mid = (bid + ask) / 2
```

The midpoint is useful for analysis, but it is not necessarily an executable price. A realistic backtest should account for crossing some or all of the spread.

### 9.2 Last price versus settlement price

The last trade is the price of the most recent transaction. It can be stale in an illiquid contract.

The settlement price is an exchange-defined value used for daily marking and final settlement processes. It can differ from the final trade, bid, ask, or midpoint.

For end-of-day futures research, settlement is often more consistent than last trade. The project should retain both when available and state which one each calculation uses.

### 9.3 Volume versus open interest

**Volume** counts contracts traded during a period. It measures activity.

**Open interest** counts contracts that remain open. It measures outstanding positions, not the number of unique traders and not directional sentiment by itself.

High volume does not necessarily increase open interest. A trade can open two positions, close two positions, or transfer risk between an opening and a closing participant.

## 10. VIX Options

### 10.1 Option basics

A **call option** gives its holder the right to receive a positive payoff when the settlement value is above the strike, subject to the contract terms.

A **put option** gives its holder the right to receive a positive payoff when the settlement value is below the strike.

At expiration, simplified cash-settled payoffs are:

```text
call payoff = max(settlement - strike, 0) * multiplier
put payoff  = max(strike - settlement, 0) * multiplier
```

The buyer pays a premium. The seller receives the premium and accepts the corresponding obligation.

### 10.2 VIX option characteristics

VIX options are cash-settled and use European-style exercise, which means they can generally be exercised only at expiration. Their final settlement is linked to the VIX SOQ process.

The standard VIX option multiplier is $100. A quoted option premium of 2.50 therefore represents $250 per contract before fees:

```text
2.50 * $100 = $250
```

Always verify current Cboe specifications before using these values in production software.

### 10.3 Why VIX options follow futures more than spot VIX

A VIX option expires against a future settlement value. The relevant VIX future for the same expiration is therefore a more appropriate forward reference than today's spot VIX.

Suppose:

- Spot VIX is 15.
- The future corresponding to an option expiration is 19.
- The option strike is 18.

Calling the 18 call “out of the money” only because 18 exceeds spot VIX can be misleading. Relative to the 19 futures level, the strike is below the forward reference.

This is a central concept for any VIX options project.

### 10.4 Option terminology

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Strike | Settlement level at which option payoff begins |
| Premium | Price paid by the option buyer |
| Expiration | Date on which the option ends and settles |
| Moneyness | Relationship between strike and the relevant underlying or forward value |
| Intrinsic value | Immediate payoff implied by current reference value |
| Time value | Premium above intrinsic value |
| Implied volatility | Volatility input consistent with the market premium |

### 10.5 The Greeks

The **Greeks** describe model-based sensitivities:

- **Delta:** sensitivity to a change in the relevant underlying or futures price.
- **Gamma:** sensitivity of delta to a change in the underlying price.
- **Theta:** sensitivity to the passage of time, all else equal.
- **Vega:** sensitivity to a change in implied volatility.
- **Rho:** sensitivity to interest rates.

For VIX options, interpretation requires care because the underlying volatility index and its futures have unusual dynamics. Greeks depend on the model, forward input, and volatility assumptions.

### 10.6 Common option positions

These descriptions explain exposures, not recommendations.

**Long call**

- Pays a premium upfront.
- Benefits from a sufficiently high settlement value.
- Maximum loss is normally the premium paid.
- Can lose money even if spot VIX rises, if the relevant forward does not rise enough or time value declines.

**Long put**

- Pays a premium upfront.
- Benefits from a sufficiently low settlement value.
- Maximum loss is normally the premium paid.
- Often affected by an upward-sloping VIX futures curve.

**Long straddle**

- Buys a call and put with the same strike and expiration.
- Benefits from a large move in either direction at settlement.
- Loses from insufficient movement and time decay.

**Long strangle**

- Buys an out-of-the-money call and put with different strikes.
- Usually costs less than a comparable straddle.
- Requires a larger move to become profitable.

**Vertical spread**

- Buys one option and sells another of the same type and expiration at a different strike.
- Limits both potential gain and loss relative to an outright option.

**Calendar spread**

- Uses options with different expirations.
- Expresses a view on the volatility term structure and timing.
- Has complex exposure because each expiration references a different VIX forward.

**Short option positions**

- Receive premium upfront.
- Can have substantial or extreme losses.
- Require careful margin, gap-risk, and settlement analysis.

## 11. Actions a Volatility Project Can Analyze

This section translates market concepts into possible project functions.

### 11.1 Observe the current market

Action:

- Load spot VIX and the active VIX futures chain.
- Display bid, ask, last, settlement, volume, open interest, and expiration.

What it represents:

- The current level of expected near-term volatility.
- Market prices for future VIX settlement dates.
- Current curve shape and liquidity.

This is the best first feature because every later calculation depends on clean contract identification and prices.

### 11.2 Classify contango and backwardation

Action:

- Compare each contract with the next contract.
- Label curve segments as contango, flat, or backwardation.

What it represents:

- How near-term volatility risk is priced relative to later risk.
- Whether stress is concentrated near the present or priced farther into the future.

This is a description of market prices, not a complete forecast.

### 11.3 Calculate curve slopes

Action:

- Calculate `F2 - F1`, `F2 / F1 - 1`, and slopes adjusted for expiration spacing.

What it represents:

- The steepness of selected curve sections.
- Changes in the market's relative pricing across horizons.

The project should store both raw and normalized values.

### 11.4 Calculate spot-futures basis

Action:

- Compare spot VIX with each futures contract.

What it represents:

- The difference between today's constant 30-day implied-volatility measure and prices for future settlement dates.

The horizons are different, so the basis is not a risk-free arbitrage signal.

### 11.5 Monitor liquidity

Action:

- Track bid-ask spread, volume, and open interest by contract.

What it represents:

- Where market activity is concentrated.
- Which contracts can support more realistic execution assumptions.
- When the dominant contract shifts as expiration approaches.

This helps define a roll rule based on expiry, volume, or open interest.

### 11.6 Reconstruct historical curves

Action:

- Store the price and metadata of every active individual contract for each observation date.

What it represents:

- The complete historical state of the curve.
- How markets priced volatility before, during, and after events.

This is more reliable than using only `VXc1` and `VXc2` continuation series.

### 11.7 Backtest a rolling futures strategy

Action:

- Define contract weights and an explicit roll schedule.
- Calculate daily marked-to-market returns.
- Include spread and transaction-cost assumptions.

What it represents:

- The return of a reproducible futures strategy rather than the movement of a continuation chart.

A valid backtest must prevent look-ahead bias and use information available at each historical timestamp.

### 11.8 Detect volatility regimes

Action:

- Classify observations using VIX level, curve slope, recent realized volatility, and rate of change.

Possible descriptive regimes:

- Low volatility with contango
- Rising volatility with flattening curve
- High volatility with backwardation
- Falling volatility after a shock

What it represents:

- A compact description of market conditions.

Regime labels are model choices. They are not natural categories discovered without assumptions.

### 11.9 Compare implied and realized volatility

Action:

- Compare a forward-looking implied measure with subsequently realized S&P 500 volatility over an aligned period.

What it represents:

- The realized outcome relative to the risk priced earlier.
- A historical estimate of the volatility risk premium.

The calculation must avoid comparing a present implied value with past realized volatility and calling the difference a forecast error.

### 11.10 Study event risk

Action:

- Examine VIX futures and options around elections, central-bank meetings, inflation releases, or earnings-heavy periods.

What it represents:

- Whether the market concentrates expected volatility around specific dates.

Weekly futures and short-horizon volatility indices can be useful, but liquidity and data coverage must be checked.

### 11.11 Build alerts

Possible alerts include:

- Front curve enters backwardation.
- Normalized `F2/F1` slope moves beyond a historical percentile.
- VIX rises rapidly while futures lag.
- Bid-ask spread becomes unusually wide.
- Volume or open interest shifts to the next contract.

An alert reports a defined condition. It does not, by itself, establish a profitable action.

- Intraday bars
- Tick-level or time-and-sales data, subject to product and entitlement

History depth, row limits, and available fields vary by service. Historical daily access does not guarantee access to real-time quotes or full historical order-book data.

### 12.5 Reference data

Reference data describes the contract rather than its changing market price. It can include:

- Expiration date
- Contract month
- Exchange
- Currency
- Contract description
- Instrument status
- Tick and multiplier information, where available

Reference data is necessary for calculating days to expiration and preventing contracts from being mixed incorrectly.

## 15. Common Research Errors

### 15.1 Treating VIX as a price forecast

Wrong interpretation:

> VIX at 20 means the S&P 500 will fall 20%.

Better interpretation:

> VIX at 20 is an annualized 30-day expected-volatility measure derived from SPX option prices.

### 15.2 Treating spot VIX as tradable

A backtest cannot assume it buys spot VIX at `.VIX`. It must use a tradable instrument such as a future or option and model that instrument's behavior.

### 15.3 Using continuation prices as strategy returns

A continuation RIC helps with charting. It does not define contract weights, roll dates, or transaction costs. Use individual contracts for a strategy backtest.

### 15.4 Ignoring expiration alignment

The front contract can have two days to expiration in one observation and 30 days in another. Comparing them without days-to-expiration controls mixes different risks.

### 15.5 Comparing last trades across illiquid contracts

A stale last price can create a false curve kink. Prefer settlement for end-of-day work and inspect bid, ask, and timestamp for current analysis.

### 15.6 Assuming open interest is bullish or bearish

Every futures contract has both a long and a short. Open interest measures outstanding contracts, not net market direction.

### 15.7 Ignoring look-ahead bias

A historical model must not use today's chain composition, revised data, or future volume to decide what it would have held in the past.

### 15.8 Ignoring survivorship and symbol changes

RIC conventions and chains can change. The VIX chain convention changed in 2012. Historical extraction must preserve identifiers and account for such changes.

### 15.9 Ignoring market-data timestamps

Spot VIX, futures, S&P 500 prices, and settlement values can refer to different times. A project must store time zone and timestamp information and avoid combining asynchronous observations as if they were simultaneous.

### 15.10 Assuming a statistical relationship is stable

VIX behavior changes across calm periods, crises, policy regimes, and market-structure changes. Historical correlation does not guarantee future behavior.

## 17. Glossary

**Annualized volatility:** Volatility expressed as an equivalent yearly rate.

**Ask:** Lowest displayed price at which a seller currently offers to sell.

**Backwardation:** Curve condition where a nearer future is above a later future.

**Basis:** Difference between a futures price and a chosen reference, often spot.

**Bid:** Highest displayed price at which a buyer currently offers to buy.

**Calendar spread:** Combined long and short positions in different expirations.

**Carry:** Gain or loss associated with holding a position as time passes, under a defined set of unchanged conditions.

**Cash settlement:** Settlement through a cash payment rather than delivery of a physical asset.

**Chain RIC:** LSEG identifier that returns a list of related instruments.

**Contango:** Curve condition where a later future is above a nearer future.

**Continuation RIC:** Rolling identifier that changes its referenced contract according to a rule.

**Convexity:** Nonlinear sensitivity where exposure changes as the market moves.

**Expiration:** Date when a derivative contract ends and settles.

**Futures curve:** Prices of futures contracts ordered by expiration date.

**Implied volatility:** Volatility value inferred from an option's market price.

**Margin:** Collateral required to support a leveraged position.

**Mark to market:** Daily or intraday recognition of position gains and losses.

**Midpoint:** Average of bid and ask.

**Open interest:** Number of futures or option contracts that remain open.

**Realized volatility:** Volatility calculated from observed historical returns.

**RIC:** Reuters Instrument Code used by LSEG to identify data instruments.

**Roll:** Replacement of an expiring contract with a later contract.

**Roll yield:** Return component associated with maintaining exposure across expiring futures, under a stated methodology.

**Settlement price:** Exchange-defined value used to mark or settle a contract.

**SOQ:** Special Opening Quotation used for final settlement of VIX derivatives.

**Term structure:** Values for the same type of risk across different future horizons.

**Tick:** Minimum permitted price increment.

**VIX:** Cboe index measuring 30-day expected S&P 500 volatility from option prices.

**Volume:** Number of contracts traded during a period.

**VVIX:** Index designed to measure expected volatility of VIX using VIX options.
